On 16 January 2003, 81.7 seconds after the Space Shuttle Columbia launched, a piece of insulating foam came off its external fuel tank and, two-tenths of a second later, struck the leading edge of the left wing ([p. 11](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=81-7-seconds-after-launch)). NASA's photo analysts found it the next day on higher-resolution film of the launch ([p. 37](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=done-after-every-launch-within&h=|they%20noticed%20a%20debris%20strike%20at%2081.9%20seconds%20after%20launch|)). The question for the next week was whether anyone would take a picture of the wing.

On 1 February Columbia broke apart over Texas during re-entry, and the seven astronauts aboard died. The Columbia Accident Investigation Board reported in August 2003; Chapter 6 rebuilds the flight from emails and logs, printed with their times. It counts three requests for pictures ([p. 152](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=conversations-log-entries-flight-day)), the first made the day the strike was found ([p. 140](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=soon-columbia-reached-orbit-morning)). This is the story of the third request, and of the week in which all three were answered.

## Tuesday 21 January

That afternoon the Debris Assessment Team, engineers from NASA, Boeing and United Space Alliance, met for two hours and agreed to ask for images of the wing from outside NASA. The mission's managers had not taken part in the meeting ([p. 151](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=after-two-hours-discussing-crater)), so the team sent its request up through engineering, via its NASA co-chair, Rodney Rocha, rather than through the mission's chain of command. The Board found that route “led in part to it being viewed by Shuttle Program managers as a non-critical engineering desire rather than a critical operational need” ([p. 152](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=routing-request-through-engineering-department&h=|a%20non-critical%20engineering%20desire%20rather%20than%20a%20critical%20operational%20need|)). At 4:41 p.m. Rocha emailed his managers: “Can we petition (beg) for outside agency assistance?” ([p. 151](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=paul-dave-meeting-participants-boeing&h=|Can%20we%20petition%20%28beg%29%20for%20outside%20agency%20assistance%3F|))

## Wednesday 22 January

That morning a Shuttle Program manager, Wayne Hale, asked a Department of Defense representative to start planning to photograph Columbia in orbit, without the authorisation of the Mission Management Team's chair, Linda Ham ([p. 152](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=morning-flight-day-seven-wayne)). At 8:30 a.m. NASA's liaison officer called and cancelled. “The NASA request to the Department of Defense to prepare to image Columbia on-orbit was both made and rescinded within 90 minutes.” ([p. 153](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=8-30-m-nasa-department&h=|The%20NASA%20request%20to%20the%20Department%20of%20Defense%20to%20prepare%20to%20image%20Columbia%20on-orbit%20was%20both%20made%20and%20rescinded%20within%2090%20minutes.|))

At 11:45 a.m. Rocha's manager, Paul Shack, emailed him that, according to the mission's evaluation room, the Orbiter Project was “not requesting any outside imaging help” ([p. 156](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=fyi-according-mer-ralph-roe&h=|not%20requesting%20any%20outside%20imaging%20help|)).

That afternoon the Debris Assessment Team met again, and was told imagery would be sought only for a “mandatory need”. “Most team members, when asked by the Board what "mandatory need" meant, replied with a shrug of their shoulders.” The Board goes on: “Analysts on the Debris Assessment Team were in the unenviable position of wanting images to more accurately assess damage while simultaneously needing to prove to Program managers, as a result of their assessment, that there was a need for images in the first place.” ([p. 157](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=not-knowing-case-debris-assessment&h=|replied%20with%20a%20shrug%20of%20their%20shoulders|))

After the meeting Rocha read Shack's email, phoned him, and sent several emails of questions. Then he wrote one he did not send; he printed it and showed it to a colleague (SSP is the Shuttle Program, MMT its Mission Management Team). It is on [p. 157](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=my-humble-technical-opinion-wrong&h=|In%20my%20humble%20technical%20opinion%2C%20this%20is%20the%20wrong%20%28and%20bordering%20on%20irresponsible%29%20answer%20from%20the%20SSP%20and%20Orbiter%20not%20to%20request%20additional%20imaging%20help%20from%20any%20outside%20source.|):

> In my humble technical opinion, this is the wrong (and bordering on irresponsible) answer from the SSP and Orbiter not to request additional imaging help from any outside source. I must emphasize (again) that severe enough damage (3 or 4 multiple tiles knocked out down to the densification layer) combined with the heating and resulting damage to the underlying structure at the most critical location (viz., MLG door/wheels/tires/hydraulics or the X1191 spar cap) could present potentially grave hazards. The engineering team will admit it might not achieve definitive high confidence answers without additional images, but, without action to request help to clarify the damage visually, we will guarantee it will not. Can we talk to Frank Benz before Fridayʼs MMT? Remember the NASA safety posters everywhere around stating, ʻIf itʼs not safe, say soʼ? Yes, itʼs that serious.

([p. 157](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=my-humble-technical-opinion-wrong)) “When asked why he did not send this e-mail, Rocha replied that he did not want to jump the chain of command.” ([p. 157](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=when-asked-why-did-not&h=|he%20did%20not%20want%20to%20jump%20the%20chain%20of%20command|))

## Thursday 23 January

The next morning Barbara Conte, of Mission Operations, called Rocha back and offered to pursue a request through her own channels. He said no: Program managers would still have to support it, and they had decided ([p. 158](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=conte-explained-rocha-mission-operations)). Later that day she took it to Leroy Cain, the flight director for re-entry, who emailed at 12:07 p.m.: “The SSP was asked directly if they had any interest/desire in requesting resources outside of NASA to view the Orbiter (ref. the wing leading edge debris concern). They said, No. After talking to Phil, I consider it to be a dead issue.” ([p. 158](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=said-no-after-talking-phil&h=|They%20said%2C%20No.%20After%20talking%20to%20Phil%2C%20I%20consider%20it%20to%20be%20a%20dead%20issue.|)) Phil is Phil Engelauf of the flight director's office, whom Cain had checked with first.

## Why I think this is worth reading

I don't read these pages as the story of one engineer who should have pressed send. Rocha had asked, in writing, through the channel he had, and the report records the answer he got. Nor of managers who ignored the danger: they spent that Wednesday asking one another, by email, whether foam could do serious damage, and the Board's comment ([p. 156](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=board-notes-these-e-mail-exchanges&h=|Here%2C%20all%20the%20key%20managers%20were%20asking%20the%20right%20question%20and%20admitting%20the%20danger.|)) is: “Here, all the key managers were asking the right question and admitting the danger.” Read in order with their times, the emails show a request moving between people who each saw a different part of it, and the sum was that nobody took the picture. The engineers were asked to prove, from an analysis they could not finish without images, that images were needed. The managers' chair asked who had a “requirement” for pictures and was told nobody did ([p. 153](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=8-30-m-nasa-department)); and the one request that had reached the military was made without authorisation, so the authority it bypassed could withdraw it.

That is why this chapter is read far outside spaceflight. The whole exchange survives with its timestamps, so you can watch a doubt that was written down move through an organisation and get answered, without ever reaching anywhere it could change the outcome.
