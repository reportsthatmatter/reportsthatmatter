Seven crew died when Challenger broke apart in January 1986. The House Science Committee's report on the accident, published that October, reprints on p. 59 a weekly activities report that an engineer at Morton Thiokol, the company that made the shuttle's booster rockets, had filed on 1 October 1985. It begins: "HELP! The seal task force is constantly being delayed by every possible means." The seals were the O-rings.

Twenty-six years on, Lord Justice Leveson finished his inquiry into the British press. On the self-regulation that had collapsed with the phone-hacking scandal, he wrote, on p. 1515: "Although the system as constituted in this manner unravelled, in spectacular fashion, in July 2011, the inherent weakness was there for all to see almost from the very start."

https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/uk-leveson-inquiry?p=1-2-follows-from-criticisms

Read enough of these reports and the same kind of thing keeps turning up in the record: a memo, a complaint, a test result, dated years before. The Post Office Horizon IT Inquiry's first volume (July 2025), which covers the human impact and redress (who was responsible is for later volumes), records at para 1.11: "Almost as soon as Legacy Horizon had been rolled out, postmasters began to complain that Horizon was, on occasions, producing false data which affected branch accounts."

## What these reports are

After a disaster or a scandal, a country sometimes appoints people with the power to compel evidence and asks them to find out what happened. The big inquiries take years, read hundreds of thousands of documents and hear hundreds of witnesses, and then publish what they found with the evidence attached. They are the most careful fact-finding a country pays for, and the fullest account anyone will write of how a tower came to be wrapped in combustible cladding, or how a bank the size of Lehman Brothers failed.

Then they disappear from view. A report gets one news day, and the news and the response to it are built from the executive summary. The rest, which in Leveson's case is 983,000 words, goes into a PDF, and the PDF sits on a website that is wound up when the inquiry closes. If it survives, it survives in a web archive. The Iraq Inquiry's web edition of Chilcot is in the UK Government Web Archive. The CIA's web pages for the Duelfer report on Iraq's weapons no longer exist except in the Wayback Machine. The Stern Review is a set of PDFs on a 2010 capture of a Treasury website. And a PDF cannot be linked to by the paragraph, so even the people who do read these reports cannot easily point anyone else at what they found.

I find these reports absorbing, and I want other people to be able to read them as I do: the text itself, with its evidence, as the inquiry published it, rather than a summary or somebody's verdict.

## What we are doing

Reports that Matter puts these reports on the web as pages you can read, search and link to. Every paragraph has its own address, with its page number or the inquiry's own paragraph number. Select any sentence and you get a link to exactly those words, so when you tell someone what a report says, you can show them. One search runs across all of the reports. The official PDF is one click from every page, and where our text and the PDF differ, the PDF is right; we say so, and we list what we have fixed. Every report's full text is also [on GitHub](https://github.com/reportsthatmatter) as plain Markdown, for anyone who wants the raw text. At the top of each report is a short introduction. That is ours, labelled as ours, and below it is the report.

How the text gets off the PDF intact is a story of its own, and a dull one unless you like PDFs. The [About page](/about) has it.

## A decade, on and off

I have wanted something like this since I was a teenager. South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission published its report online, and anyone could read it. It showed me that putting the record where everyone can read it is part of how a country reckons with what happened to it.

In 2014 and 2015 I started building it, under the name Official Inquiries. It came from the same conviction that had got me to start the Open Knowledge Foundation: information a society has already paid for should be usable, not merely available. In 2016 and 2017 we built a prototype at Datopian. Then the funding ran out and the project stopped. My note from 2017 says "Official Inquiries => does not mean anything to most people". A later one just says "Needs funding and focus." For seven years it had neither.

What changed is that the work a funded team was needed for can now be done by one person with very little time, with AI coding agents doing the building. I should be honest about what that means. Each report is still weeks of work: getting a million words off the page intact, then checking the text against the PDF, finding where a footnote marker has been read as a number or a heading has gone missing, and fixing it, by hand and with the agents, until the report is fit to read. Nothing went up in an afternoon. In December 2024 I renamed the project Reports that Matter, and through 2025 and 2026 the reports went up one by one. One rule has not changed: no language model wrote, summarised or rewrote the report text. AI coding agents built much of the software and the site, under my direction, and the code is public. The introductions are ours: drafted with AI help, checked by me, and labelled.

## What is there

There are 24 reports, from the UK and the US, published between 1986 and 2026. In date order: the House Science Committee's Challenger report (1986); the Columbia Accident Investigation Board (2003); the 9/11 Commission (2004); the Duelfer report on Iraq's weapons programmes (2004); *United States v. Philip Morris* (2006); the Stern Review on the economics of climate change (2006); the Lehman Brothers examiner's report (2010); the Saville Inquiry into Bloody Sunday (2010); the Deepwater Horizon commission (2011); the Senate's financial-crisis report (2011); the Hillsborough Independent Panel (2012); Leveson (2012); the Senate torture report (2014); the Litvinenko Inquiry (2016); Chilcot (2016); the 2017 Intelligence Community Assessment of Russian election interference; Mueller (2019); the January 6th Committee (2022); Grenfell Phase 2 (2024); Jack Smith (2025); the Post Office Horizon IT Inquiry (2025); the Covid Inquiry's Module 2 report (2025); the Southport Inquiry's Phase 1 report (2026); and the Thirlwall Inquiry into the Countess of Chester Hospital (2026).

Some are not whole yet, and each says so at the top. Chilcot is the executive summary; Lehman is Volumes 1 and 3; Saville, Columbia and Duelfer are Volume I; Jack Smith, Southport and the Post Office are Volume 1. The torture report is the declassified summary and findings; the full study of more than 6,700 pages is still classified.

Next are the later volumes of those, and whichever reports people keep asking for. If there is one you need, write to contact@reportsthatmatter.org. The [Bluesky account](https://bsky.app/profile/reportsthatmatter.org) posts one passage a day, verbatim, with its page and its link, and once a month the [newsletter](/newsletter) gathers a few passages from the archive on a single thread.
