"Doubt is our product since it is the best means of competing with the 'body of fact' that exists in the mind of the general public. It is also the means of establishing a controversy." That is from an internal Brown & Williamson paper called "Smoking and Health Proposal". It is quoted on [p. 1537](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=paramount-significance-defendants-internal-documents) of *United States v. Philip Morris*, the 2006 opinion in which a federal judge in Washington, Gladys Kessler, found that the major American cigarette companies had deceived the public about smoking for at least 50 years. The opinion runs to 1,682 pages, and much of it is the companies' own files, quoted with their document numbers. The same paragraph has the Tobacco Institute's procedural memo of 1968: "The most important type of story is that which casts doubt on the cause and effect theory of disease and smoking. . . . [T]he headline should strongly call out the point – Controversy! Contradiction! Other factors! Unknowns!" This is why the case matters: it is the inside record of how an industry handled science it privately accepted. The answer was not denial, which could be tested, but doubt, which could be manufactured, and here is the method being planned on paper decades before anyone outside saw it.

The 9/11 Commission (2004) was given more than 2.5 million pages of documents and more than 1,000 hours of audiotape, and from them it rebuilt the morning of 11 September 2001 to the minute. Its account of the response to the first hijacked flight by NEADS, NORAD's Northeast Air Defense Sector, the military post responsible for that airspace, ends on [p. 21](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-911-commission?p=summary-neads-received-notice-hijacking): "In summary, NEADS received notice of the hijacking nine minutes before it struck the North Tower. That nine minutes' notice before impact was the most the military would receive of any of the four hijackings." The question asked for years afterwards was whether the planes could have been stopped. The record answers it: the air defences had been built for an attack arriving from outside the country.

The Senate's financial-crisis report (2011) came out of a two-year investigation with subpoenas and tens of millions of pages of documents. On [p. 297](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-psi-financial-crisis?p=times-some-cra-analysts-openly) it quotes a Standard & Poor's analyst in a December 2006 email about a security the agency had been asked to rate: "Let's hope we are all wealthy and retired by the time this house of cards falters." And an instant message from April 2007: "[W]e rate every deal[.] [I]t could be structured by cows and we would rate it." The crisis is usually told as a failure of complexity, of instruments nobody understood. The emails are the record of people who understood them well enough, saying so to each other at the time.

None of these is a conclusion. They are the evidence, reprinted, and it is the evidence rather than the verdict that tells you what actually happened. That is what the news has no room for, and it is what this project is about.

## What these reports are

After a disaster or a scandal, a country sometimes appoints people with the power to compel evidence and asks them to find out what happened. The big inquiries take years and read hundreds of thousands of documents, and then publish what they found with the evidence attached. What comes out is the most detailed factual account of that moment anyone has assembled. The Columbia Accident Investigation Board (2003) reprints the emails that NASA managers and engineers sent one another on Wednesday 22 January 2003, 10 days before Columbia broke apart on re-entry, with their times, as a request to have the shuttle's wing photographed in orbit was raised and then cancelled. On [p. 157](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/columbia-accident?p=my-humble-technical-opinion-wrong) it prints one that an engineer, Rodney Rocha, wrote that afternoon and did not send: "In my humble technical opinion, this is the wrong (and bordering on irresponsible) answer from the SSP and Orbiter not to request additional imaging help from any outside source." What the record shows is less a single mistake than a process: the engineers asked for pictures, the managers decided the question had been answered, and the engineer with the sharpest doubt kept it to a printout because, he told the Board, he did not want to jump the chain of command. You get that from the emails, in order, with their times, not from a summary.

Then the record disappears from view. A report gets one news day, and the news and the response to it are built from the executive summary, which is the analysis. The evidence, which in Leveson's case is most of 983,000 words, goes into a PDF, and the PDF sits on a website that is often wound up when the inquiry closes. If it survives, it survives in a web archive. The Iraq Inquiry's web edition of Chilcot is in the UK Government Web Archive. The CIA's web pages for the Duelfer report on Iraq's weapons no longer exist except in the Wayback Machine. And a PDF cannot be linked to by the paragraph, so even the people who do read these reports cannot easily point anyone else at what they found.

I find these reports absorbing, and I want other people to be able to read them as I do: whole, as the inquiry published them.

## What we are doing

Reports that Matter puts these reports on the web as pages you can read, search and link to. Every paragraph has its own address, with its page number or the inquiry's own paragraph number. Select any sentence and you get a link to exactly those words, so when you tell someone what a report says, you can show them. The official PDF is one click from every page, and where our text and the PDF differ, the PDF is right; we say so, and we list what we have fixed. Every report's full text is also [on GitHub](https://github.com/reportsthatmatter) as plain Markdown.

How the text gets off the PDF intact is a story of its own, and a dull one unless you like PDFs. The [About page](/about) has it.

## A decade, on and off

I have wanted something like this since I was a teenager. South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission published its report online, and anyone could read it. It showed me that putting the record where everyone can read it is part of how a country reckons with what happened to it.

In 2014 and 2015 I started building it, under the name Official Inquiries. It came from the same conviction that had got me to start the Open Knowledge Foundation: information a society has already paid for should be usable, not merely available. In 2016 and 2017 we built a prototype at Datopian. Then the funding ran out and the project stopped. My note from 2017 says "Official Inquiries => does not mean anything to most people". A later one just says "Needs funding and focus." For 7 years it had neither.

What changed is that the work a funded team was needed for can now be done by one person with very little time. One rule has not changed: no language model wrote, summarised or rewrote the report text. AI coding agents built much of the software and the site, under my direction, and the code is public. The short introduction at the top of each report is ours: drafted with AI help, checked by me, and labelled. Each report is still weeks of work: getting up to a million words off the page intact, then checking the text against the PDF and fixing what went wrong, a footnote marker read as a number, a heading gone missing, by hand and with the agents, until the report is fit to read. Nothing went up in an afternoon. In December 2024 I renamed the project Reports that Matter, and through 2025 and 2026 the reports went up one by one.

## What is there

There are 24 reports, from the UK and the US, published between 1986 and 2026. In date order: the House Science Committee's Challenger report (1986); the Columbia Accident Investigation Board (2003); the 9/11 Commission (2004); the Duelfer report on Iraq's weapons programmes (2004); *United States v. Philip Morris* (2006); the Stern Review on the economics of climate change (2006); the Lehman Brothers examiner's report (2010); the Saville Inquiry into Bloody Sunday (2010); the Deepwater Horizon commission (2011); the Senate's financial-crisis report (2011); the Hillsborough Independent Panel (2012); Leveson (2012); the Senate torture report (2014); the Litvinenko Inquiry (2016); Chilcot (2016); the 2017 Intelligence Community Assessment of Russian election interference; Mueller (2019); the January 6th Committee (2022); Grenfell Phase 2 (2024); Jack Smith (2025); the Post Office Horizon IT Inquiry (2025); the Covid Inquiry's Module 2 report (2025); the Southport Inquiry's Phase 1 report (2026); and the Thirlwall Inquiry into the Countess of Chester Hospital (2026).

Some are not whole yet, and each says so at the top. Chilcot is the executive summary; Lehman is Volumes 1 and 3; Saville, Columbia and Duelfer are Volume I; Jack Smith, Southport and the Post Office are Volume 1. The torture report is the declassified summary and findings; the full study of more than 6,700 pages is still classified.

Next are the later volumes of those, and whichever reports people keep asking for. If there is one you need, write to contact@reportsthatmatter.org. The [Bluesky account](https://bsky.app/profile/reportsthatmatter.org) posts one passage a day, verbatim, with its page and its link, and once a month the [newsletter](/newsletter) gathers a few passages from the archive on one subject.
