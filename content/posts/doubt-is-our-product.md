“Doubt is our product since it is the best means of competing with the 'body of fact' that exists in the mind of the general public. It is also the means of establishing a controversy.” A Brown & Williamson paper titled “Smoking and Health Proposal”, quoted on [p. 1537](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=paramount-significance-defendants-internal-documents&h=|Doubt%20is%20our%20product%20since%20it%20is%20the%20best%20means%20of%20competing%20with%20the%20'body%20of%20fact'%20that%20exists%20in%20the%20mind%20of%20the%20general%20public.%20It%20is%20also%20the%20means%20of%20establishing%20a%20controversy.|) of *United States v. Philip Morris* (2006). The opinion is full of such documents. Here are six of them, in the order they were written, and first the public statement they were written behind.

On 4 January 1954 the leading American cigarette makers took out a full-page advertisement in 448 newspapers ([p. 53](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=16-following-hill-knowlton-s&h=|A%20Frank%20Statement%20to%20Cigarette%20Smokers|)): “A Frank Statement to Cigarette Smokers”, placed on the advice of the public-relations firm Hill & Knowlton. It announced a joint research committee, said that smoking was not a proven cause of lung cancer, and accepted “an interest in people's health as a basic responsibility, paramount to every other consideration in our business” ([p. 54](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=18-frank-statement-set-forth&h=|an%20interest%20in%20people's%20health%20as%20a%20basic%20responsibility%2C%20paramount%20to%20every%20other%20consideration%20in%20our%20business|)). The court calls this the industry's “open question” position, and finds it was maintained for more than forty years.

Fifty-two years later, in August 2006, Judge Gladys Kessler of the federal district court in Washington signed a 1,682-page opinion in the Justice Department's racketeering case against those companies. Behind it were seven years of litigation, a nine-month trial, 84 witnesses and the exchange of millions of documents ([p. 33](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=seven-year-history-extraordinarily-complex-case)). Most of the opinion is findings of fact, and most of the findings rest on the companies' own files.

## 1963: the general counsel's memorandum

On 17 July 1963 Addison Yeaman, general counsel of Brown & Williamson, wrote a memorandum marked “Strictly Private and Confidential” about nicotine research the company's British parent had run. The court's finding on [p. 452](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=1082-shortly-thereafter-b-w&h=|We%20are%2C%20then%2C%20in%20the%20business%20of%20selling%20nicotine%2C%20an%20addictive%20drug%20effective%20in%20the%20release%20of%20stress%20mechanisms.|) is that Yeaman concluded that “nicotine is addictive”, and went on: “We are, then, in the business of selling nicotine, an addictive drug effective in the release of stress mechanisms.” The Frank Statement was 9 years old. The court lists seven things the companies deceived the public about ([p. 1535](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=totality-evidence-proves-defendants-wide)); that nicotine was not addictive is the second.

## 1968: the Tobacco Institute, twice

The Tobacco Institute was the industry's trade body. In April 1968 ([p. 103](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=129-however-april-1968-memorandum)) its vice-president for public relations, William Kloepfer, wrote to its president. As the court quotes him on [p. 1537](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=paramount-significance-defendants-internal-documents&h=|Our%20basic%20position%20in%20the%20cigarette%20controversy%20is%20subject%20to%20the%20charge%2C%20and%20may%20be%20subject%20to%20a%20finding%2C%20that%20we%20are%20making%20false%20or%20misleading%20statements%20to%20promote%20the%20sale%20of%20cigarettes.|): “Our basic position in the cigarette controversy is subject to the charge, and may be subject to a finding, that we are making false or misleading statements to promote the sale of cigarettes.” The same year the Institute's internal “Tobacco and Health Research Procedural Memo” set out the kind of press coverage to seek: “The most important type of story is that which casts doubt on the cause and effect theory of disease and smoking. . . . [T]he headline should strongly call out the point – Controversy! Contradiction! Other factors! Unknowns!” (same page). The “Smoking and Health Proposal” sits beside them on that page; the court calls it undated and, in its findings, places it alongside the industry's public-relations plan of 1967 ([p. 331](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=726-same-time-internal-b)). It introduces all three with one sentence: the companies' internal papers “openly acknowledge the purpose of their public relations strategy”.

## 1972: the strategy, described by the people running it

On 1 May 1972 Fred Panzer, a vice-president of the Tobacco Institute, wrote to its president. The court quotes the memo on [p. 337](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=745-may-1-1972-memo&h=|creating%20doubt%20about%20the%20health%20charge%20without%20actually%20denying%20it|). “For nearly twenty years, this industry has employed a single strategy to defend itself on three major fronts -- litigation, politics, and public opinion.” It had, Panzer wrote, “always been a holding strategy consisting of” three things: “creating doubt about the health charge without actually denying it”; “advocating the public's right to smoke, without actually urging them to take up the practice”; and “encouraging objective scientific research as the only way to resolve the question of health hazard.” He was plain about what it was not: “it is not -- nor was it intended to be -- a vehicle for victory.” It had been designed for the courtroom and had won there. On the next page he reports the other front: “On the public opinion front, however, our situation has deteriorated and will continue to worsen.” ([p. 338](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=745-may-1-1972-memo&h=|On%20the%20public%20opinion%20front%2C%20however%2C%20our%20situation%20has%20deteriorated%20and%20will%20continue%20to%20worsen.|))

## 1981: “Young Smokers”

A 1981 report by the Philip Morris Research Center, titled “Young Smokers Prevalence, Trends, Implications, and Related Demographic Trends”, stated: “Today's teenager is tomorrow's potential regular customer, and the overwhelming majority of smokers first begin to smoke while still in their teens. . . . The smoking patterns of teenagers are particularly important to Philip Morris.” The court quotes it on [p. 1548](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=defendants-fraudulent-statements-stem-from&h=|Today's%20teenager%20is%20tomorrow's%20potential%20regular%20customer%2C%20and%20the%20overwhelming%20majority%20of%20smokers%20first%20begin%20to%20smoke%20while%20still%20in%20their%20teens.|), in the section on the companies' public denials that they marketed to the young.

## Why I think this is worth reading

The usual story about the tobacco industry is that it lied, and the court finds that it did: the first of its seven items is denying the health effects of smoking. What I find more useful in the record is the second thing running alongside the denials: a method, written down by its authors, for keeping a settled question open. The companies' own scientists did not believe the denials; one, formerly of Philip Morris, told the court, “I never talked with a scientist at Philip Morris who said that smoking doesn't cause disease” ([p. 1536](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=defendants-efforts-deny-distort-scientific&h=|I%20never%20talked%20with%20a%20scientist%20at%20Philip%20Morris%20who%20said%20that%20smoking%20doesn't%20cause%20disease.|)). A denial can be tested and lost. A controversy, maintained, cannot be lost in the same way; it only has to last. Panzer's memo is the record of that: a strategy built for litigation, winning there, and, by 1972, by his own account, losing with the public.

That is why I keep going back to this case: the method is in the documents, with dates, and the court lays them end to end. Its own summary, on [p. 33](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris?p=seven-year-history-extraordinarily-complex-case&h=|Defendants%20have%20known%20many%20of%20these%20facts%20for%20at%20least%2050%20years%20or%20more.|), is one sentence: “Defendants have known many of these facts for at least 50 years or more.”

The opinion is a 1,682-page PDF on the Justice Department's website; the news of the day was built on its summary. Reports that Matter puts it online as pages you can read, search and link to the paragraph, in the court's own words, with the official PDF one click away: [*United States v. Philip Morris* (2006)](https://reportsthatmatter.org/reports/us-v-philip-morris).
