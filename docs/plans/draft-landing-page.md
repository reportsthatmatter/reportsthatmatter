### Important public reports exist.

### They are just hard to find, hard to use, and hard to link to.

Governments publish vast amounts of high-quality information on matters of real public importance: official inquiries, investigations, and reports produced with serious resources, expert authorship, and often subpoena powers.

Yet much of this material is effectively hidden.

It is scattered across obscure or broken websites, locked inside huge PDF files, poorly indexed by search engines, and almost impossible to link to at the level that actually matters — the specific finding, paragraph, or piece of evidence.

Even when journalists or commentators refer to these reports, readers are rarely taken to the report itself, let alone the relevant section. The result is that some of the most careful public research ever produced is underused in public debate.

---

### Reports that Matter exists to fix this.

Reports that Matter makes important public-interest reports usable on the web.

We collect, structure, and present serious reports so that they are:

* discoverable via search,
* readable online (not just downloadable PDFs),
* linkable at the level of specific sections or passages,
* easy to browse, search, and reference.

Instead of dead-end PDFs and maze-like government websites, reports become something you can actually navigate, cite, and share.

### This is a systemic problem

Across jurisdictions and decades, the pattern repeats:

* Important reports exist.
* They are published once.
* They are poorly structured for the web.
* Over time, links rot, formats decay, and discoverability collapses.

The web excels at sharing, linking, and searching.
These reports are published as if the web did not exist.

---

### What Reports that Matter does differently

Reports that Matter applies a small set of simple principles:

* Permanent storage using modern infrastructure.
* Full-text extraction so reports are searchable.
* Deep links directly into sections and paragraphs.
* Clean, web-native presentation.

In practice, this means:

* you can link to the exact passage that matters,
* search engines can index the content properly,
* readers can browse, skim, and explore without downloading massive files.

---

### Why this matters

Official inquiry reports often contain the factual backbone of public understanding: evidence, timelines, testimony, and findings that shape how societies understand crises, failures, and institutional responsibility.

When this material is inaccessible, public discourse becomes thinner, more second-hand, and less accountable.

Making reports usable is a small but foundational step toward better-informed journalism, research, and civic debate.

---

### Scope and focus

We focus initially on English-language reports from the UK and US, where inquiry reports are often exceptionally rich but remain poorly served by existing publication practices.

Over time, the same approach can extend wherever serious public reports exist but remain locked away.
